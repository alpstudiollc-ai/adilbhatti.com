The 1942 Battle of Los Angeles Explained

Adil Bhatti · Updated 25 September 2026

Short answerIn the early hours of February 25, 1942, less than three months after Pearl Harbor, air raid sirens sounded across Los Angeles and Army anti aircraft batteries fired more than 1,400 rounds at something over the city for roughly an hour. Searchlights converged on an object that thousands of residents watched. No enemy aircraft was shot down, none was ever recovered, and the Army and Navy gave contradicting explanations within a day. The official position settled on war nerves and a stray weather balloon. The photograph published by the Los Angeles Times remains the most argued image in the history of the subject.

What happened that night

Los Angeles in February 1942 was a city expecting attack. A Japanese submarine had shelled an oil field near Santa Barbara the evening before, on February 23. Blackouts were in force. Anti aircraft guns ringed the coast.

At 2:25am on February 25, radar reported an unidentified target approaching from the sea. Sirens went off. At 3:16am the 37th Coast Artillery Brigade opened fire. The shooting lasted until about 4:14am. Searchlights held on something over Culver City and Santa Monica while shells burst around it. Residents reported one large object and several smaller ones. Five civilians died, three in car accidents in the blackout and two of heart attacks.

By morning there was no wreckage, no Japanese plane, no bomb crater, and no agreement on what had been shot at.

What the authorities said, and when

The two services contradicted each other within hours, which is part of why the case never closed.

  • Navy Secretary Frank Knox, February 25: a false alarm, caused by jittery nerves. No planes over the city.
  • Army Secretary Henry Stimson, February 26: as many as fifteen unidentified planes, possibly commercial aircraft flown by enemy agents to locate the guns.
  • Later Army review: possibly a weather balloon set off the initial alert, and the rest was over excited gunners firing at shell bursts and searchlight glare.

Japan, after the war, confirmed it had no aircraft over Los Angeles that night. That removed the enemy plane explanation and left the balloon and the nerves.

Why the case still matters

Most UFO cases rest on one or two witnesses. This one has radar, a documented military engagement, official statements from two cabinet secretaries, a coroner's count, a newspaper photograph, and a witness pool in the tens of thousands. It is one of the largest mass sightings on record, and it happened five years before Roswell, which is why serious histories start the modern era here rather than in New Mexico.

The strongest argument against an exotic explanation is also the simplest: frightened gunners under blackout, firing at their own shell bursts. The strongest argument for something unexplained is that trained artillery crews held a target in searchlights for the better part of an hour and could not bring it down. Both are in the record. The record does not settle it.

Takeaway. The Battle of Los Angeles is the earliest case where the US military engaged an unidentified object, documented it, and still could not explain it. Everything that came later, Roswell, the Nimitz encounter, the Congressional hearings, is easier to read once you have this one in place.

What people get wrong

Calling it a Japanese attack
Japan confirmed after the war that it had no aircraft over the city. The February 26 Army claim was retracted by later review.
Treating the Los Angeles Times photo as proof
The published photo was retouched for print, as was standard then. The original negative shows a less dramatic image. It is evidence of the event, not of what the object was.
Confusing it with the Santa Barbara shelling
The oil field attack on February 23 was a real Japanese submarine action. The Los Angeles firing two nights later was not connected to any confirmed enemy.
Assuming it was quickly forgotten
It was investigated, debated in Congress, and reviewed by the Army. The contradicting official statements are why it is still cited.
Placing it after Roswell
It happened in 1942, five years before Roswell. It is the first major US military engagement with an unidentified object.

Questions and answers

How many shells were fired during the Battle of Los Angeles?
Army records put the count at more than 1,400 anti aircraft rounds, fired between roughly 3:16am and 4:14am on February 25, 1942. Nothing was hit and nothing fell out of the sky.
Did anyone die in the Battle of Los Angeles?
Five civilians. Three in traffic accidents during the blackout and two of heart attacks attributed to the stress of the raid. Nobody was hurt by enemy action, because no enemy was ever confirmed present.
Was the Battle of Los Angeles a real Japanese attack?
No. Japan confirmed after the war that it had no aircraft over Los Angeles that night. The Army's initial claim of enemy planes was later withdrawn.
What did the Los Angeles Times photo actually show?
Searchlight beams converging on a point in the sky with shell bursts around it. The version printed on February 26, 1942 was retouched for reproduction, which was normal practice at the time. The original negative is less dramatic.
What is the official explanation for the Battle of Los Angeles?
The final Army position was that a weather balloon likely triggered the initial alert and that nervous gunners then fired at shell bursts and searchlight reflections. The Navy had already called it a false alarm. The two services never fully reconciled.
Why do UFO researchers care about the 1942 Los Angeles incident?
Because it has radar, an hour of documented military fire, official statements from two cabinet secretaries, a coroner's count, and tens of thousands of witnesses. It is one of the largest mass sightings in the record and it predates Roswell by five years.

The 1979 Steven Spielberg comedy 1941 is loosely inspired by the panic of that week. The 2011 film Battle: Los Angeles borrows the name but is unrelated science fiction.
Where was the object seen over Los Angeles in 1942?
Reports centred on the coast from Santa Monica through Culver City toward Long Beach, with searchlights holding on a point over the Santa Monica and Culver City area for the longest stretch.
