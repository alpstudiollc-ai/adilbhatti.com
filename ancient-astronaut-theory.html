Ancient Astronaut Theory: What It Claims, What It Does Not

Adil Bhatti · Updated 16 October 2026

Short answerAncient astronaut theory, also called paleocontact, is the idea that intelligent beings from somewhere other than Earth visited in antiquity, and that the visit survives in the record: in texts that describe beings coming down from the sky, in monuments aligned to the stars with a precision their builders are not supposed to have had, and in art that looks like nothing else from its period. The popular version, from Erich von Däniken's 1968 book Chariots of the Gods? and the History channel's Ancient Aliens, which premiered April 20, 2010, says the visitors built the monuments. The serious version claims something narrower: that certain texts and artifacts describe something, and that the standard explanations for them are weaker than they look. The two versions use the same examples and should not be confused.

Where the theory came from

Three dates matter. In 1966 the astronomers Carl Sagan and Iosif Shklovskii published Intelligent Life in the Universe, which treated contact with Earth in the distant past as a hypothesis worth examining. In 1968 Erich von Däniken published Chariots of the Gods?, which turned the hypothesis into a bestseller and a brand. In 1976 Zecharia Sitchin published The 12th Planet, which read the Sumerian tablets as a record of visitors called the Anunnaki. Ancient Aliens, the television series, arrived on April 20, 2010 and has run ever since.

By 1979 Sagan was the theory's best known critic, in Broca's Brain. That arc, from a hypothesis a serious astronomer would entertain to a thing serious people felt obliged to distance themselves from, happened in thirteen years, and the examples never changed. What changed was who was making the argument and how carefully.

What it claims, and what it does not

The popular version claims that visitors built or directed the building of the great monuments, that they were worshipped as gods, and that the texts describing gods in the sky are literal reports. That is the version on television, and it is the version archaeologists reject, with reason, because it assumes the people who built those monuments could not have done it themselves.

The serious version does not claim that. It makes no argument about who moved the stones. It asks what specific texts and artifacts actually describe, and whether the conventional reading accounts for all of it. Those are different claims. The first can be dismissed by showing how a pyramid was built. The second cannot, because it was never about the building.

What skeptics say, and what a serious reader does with it

Archaeologists make three objections, and they are good ones. The theory underestimates ancient people, who were as intelligent as we are and had centuries to practice. It cherry picks, lifting an odd carving out of a culture whose other ten thousand carvings are ordinary. And it argues from resemblance: this looks like a helmet, that looks like a rocket, as if looking like something were evidence of it.

A serious reader accepts all three and then notices what they cover. They answer the building question completely. They answer the resemblance question completely. They do not answer the text question, because a text is not a resemblance. When a tablet, a prophet, or a scripture describes beings descending in a vehicle, the question is not what it looks like but what the writer meant, and that is a question for translators and historians, not engineers. Which of the seven threads survive that test, and which do not, is the part this guide leaves for the book.

Takeaway. The pyramid argument has been settled for decades. The text argument has not, because it was never the same argument. Knowing which one you are having is most of the work.

What people get wrong

Treating the television series as the theory
Ancient Aliens is one version, the loudest one. The theory is older than the show and narrower than the show in its serious form.
Saying the theory claims aliens built the pyramids
The popular version does. The serious version makes no claim about construction at all. It is about what the texts and artifacts describe.
Assuming the skeptics have closed the whole question
They have closed the engineering question. The text question is a different kind of question and needs a different kind of answer. The book takes it thread by thread.
Calling it a religious claim
It is a claim about the historical record. The book notes that confirmation would bear on religious assumptions, which is a consequence, not the claim.
Thinking the subject ended with von Däniken
The Pentagon has been releasing UAP records since May 2026. The book's argument is that the ancient end and the modern end are one record, and that reading either without the other is how people get it wrong.

Questions and answers

Is ancient astronaut theory the same as paleocontact?
Yes. Paleocontact is the academic term, used by Sagan and Shklovskii in 1966, for contact between Earth and an outside intelligence in the distant past. Ancient astronaut theory is the popular name for the same idea, and it picked up the construction claims, who built the pyramids and the Nazca Lines, that the academic term never carried.
Who started ancient astronaut theory?
Charles Fort raised the idea in The Book of the Damned in 1919. Carl Sagan and Iosif Shklovskii treated it as a hypothesis in Intelligent Life in the Universe in 1966. Erich von Däniken made it famous with Chariots of the Gods? in 1968, and Zecharia Sitchin gave it the Anunnaki with The 12th Planet in 1976.
Is ancient astronaut theory the same thing as the Ancient Aliens TV show?
No. The show, which premiered on History on April 20, 2010, presents the popular version, in which visitors built the monuments. The theory predates the show by decades, and its serious form makes a much narrower claim about what ancient texts and artifacts describe.
What is the difference between the popular version and the serious version?
The popular version argues about construction: who built the pyramids, who drew the Nazca Lines. The serious version argues about description: what the Sumerian tablets, Ezekiel, the Hindu scriptures, and the Quran actually say about beings from the sky, and whether the conventional readings account for all of it. The first can be answered by engineering. The second cannot.
Does ancient astronaut theory say aliens built the pyramids?
The television version does. The serious version does not, and Ancient Aliens to Modern UFOs does not make that claim. Its Egyptian chapter is about monuments aligned to the stars, and what the alignment data shows is in the book, not here.
Why are the Anunnaki central to ancient astronaut theory?
Because Zecharia Sitchin's 1976 book The 12th Planet read the Sumerian tablets as a record of visitors from another world, and most modern ancient astronaut claims descend from that reading. The tablets are the oldest of the seven threads in Ancient Aliens to Modern UFOs. How far Sitchin's translation can be trusted, and what the tablets say without it, is in the book.
Why do people connect Ezekiel's vision to UFOs?
Ezekiel 1, written around 593 BCE, describes a vehicle of wheels within wheels, fire, and living creatures descending from the north. In 1974 Josef Blumrich, a NASA engineer who set out to debunk the reading, published The Spaceships of Ezekiel arguing the text described a craft. The guide names the thread. The line by line reading is in the book.
What three questions does the book ask of each ancient thread?
What the text or artifact actually says or shows. What the standard explanation for it is. And what that explanation leaves out. Every one of the seven threads, from the Sumerian tablets to the Nazca Lines, goes through the same three questions in order, which is what separates the book from the television version.
Is ancient astronaut theory a religious claim?
No. It is a claim about the historical record: what certain ancient texts and objects describe. Ancient Aliens to Modern UFOs notes that confirmed contact would bear on assumptions in most religious traditions and in the secular account of human origins, but that is a consequence of the claim, not the claim itself.
What do archaeologists say about ancient astronaut theory?
They reject the popular version, on three grounds: it underestimates ancient people, it cherry picks unusual objects out of ordinary cultures, and it argues from resemblance. All three objections are sound against the construction claim. Whether they reach the text claim is the question the book takes up.
Did Carl Sagan believe in ancient astronauts?
In 1966 he co-wrote a book that treated past contact as a hypothesis worth examining. By 1979, in Broca's Brain, he was the sharpest public critic of von Däniken. He did not change his mind about the hypothesis so much as about the way it was being argued.
What are the seven ancient threads in Ancient Aliens to Modern UFOs?
The Sumerian tablets and the Anunnaki, Egyptian monuments aligned to the stars, the Amarna sculptures from Akhenaten's reign with their elongated skulls, Ezekiel's wheels within wheels, Hindu scripture on celestial beings and their vehicles, Quranic references to celestial beings, and the Nazca Lines. The guide names them. What each one says, and what survives the standard explanation, is the first part of the book.
